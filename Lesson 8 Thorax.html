Orientation

What should you be able to explain?

The goal is not to memorize every label. It is to understand how plates, ridges, joints, and muscles form a coherent mechanical system.

1

Recognize the plan

Identify the notum, pleuron, sternum, coxa, and the major internal apodemes of a thoracic segment.

2

Connect form to function

Explain how the pleural ridge, wing processes, phragmata, and furca transmit forces from muscles to legs and wings.

3

Interpret transformation

Use fusion, reduction, membranization, displacement, and functional reassignment to compare thoraces across insect groups.

The basic coordinate system

Basic organization of a body segment

A generalized insect segment is organized into a dorsal tergum, paired lateral pleura, and a ventral sternum.

Tergum
The dorsal region or principal dorsal sclerite of a segment; in the thorax it is commonly called the notum.
Pleuron
The lateral body-wall region between the tergum and sternum; in the thorax it supports the appendage base and becomes highly reinforced.
Sternum
The ventral region or principal ventral sclerite of a segment, providing protection and muscle attachment below the body.
Sclerite
A locally hardened plate of cuticle; adjacent sclerites may be separated by sutures or flexible membrane.
Haemocoel
The main internal body cavity containing haemolymph and the visceral organs.
Function

The tergum resists dorsal loads, the pleura transmit appendage forces, and the sternum braces the ventral surface.

Evolution

Fusion, subdivision, and internal inflection transform this simple plan into the specialized thoracic skeleton.

Remember: these are body-wall regions. Their visible adult plates may not correspond exactly to the primary embryonic segment.

Reading the exoskeleton

Primary vs. secondary segmentation

The visible border between adult plates is not necessarily the original embryonic segment boundary.

`T`
Tergum/notum — the principal dorsal plate of a thoracic segment.
`atg`
Acrotergite — the narrow anterior area before the antecostal suture.
`acs`
Antecostal suture — the external groove marking the internal antecosta.
`Mb`
Intersegmental membrane — flexible cuticle between definitive plates.
Function

The membrane permits bending and telescoping while the sclerotized tergum carries muscle forces.

Evolution

Secondary segmentation reorganizes the original metameric pattern into more effective mechanical units.

From segmentation to regional specialization

Subdivision of the thoracic nota

Read the diagram vertically: it compares the dorsal plate of T1, T2, and T3. The labeled subdivisions are regions within a notum—not additional body segments.

Key regions

Prescutum
The anterior notal region; often small, reduced, or externally inconspicuous.
Scutum
The largest alinotal region and a major site for transmitting forces generated by the indirect flight muscles.
Scutellum
The posterior shield-like region, often conspicuous as a triangular or rounded plate.
Postnotum
The posterior notal region; its internal inflection may form a phragma for flight-muscle attachment.
Function

The enlarged T2 notum braces the forewing base and provides attachment surfaces for the principal flight motor.

Evolution

T1 remains comparatively simple, whereas the wing-bearing T2 and T3 nota—the alinota—became subdivided and internally reinforced. T2 is usually the more developed flight segment.

External groove, internal ridge

The antecosta converts cuticle into a muscle anchor

An external suture often reveals an internal reinforcement rather than a true separation between ancestral parts.

`Ac`
Antecosta — an internal transverse ridge beneath the antecostal suture.
`DMcl`
Dorsal longitudinal muscle — a muscle spanning successive antecostae or phragmata.
`atg`
Acrotergite — the precostal part of the definitive plate.
`Mb`
Intersegmental membrane — the flexible connection between plates.
Function

The ridge increases attachment area and transfers longitudinal muscle tension into the dorsal exoskeleton.

Evolution

In wing-bearing segments, enlarged antecostal apodemes become phragmata for powerful indirect flight muscles.

Mobility with support

The cervix links the head to the thoracic skeleton

Small cervical plates preserve head mobility while longitudinal muscles stabilize the head–thorax connection.

`Cvx`
Cervix — the flexible neck between the head and prothorax.
`1cv, 2cv`
Cervical sclerites — lateral plates forming movable support points.
`PoR`
Postoccipital ridge — an internal head ridge for muscle attachment.
`1Ph`
First phragma — the enlarged internal tergal apodeme at the anterior mesothorax.
`SA₁, SA₂`
Sternal apophyses — paired internal arms of the thoracic sterna.
`1Spn, 2Spn`
Spinae — median processes of spinasterna; they are not spiracles.
Function

Dorsal and ventral longitudinal muscles coordinate head movement and brace the anterior thorax.

Evolution

The cervix is best interpreted as an intersegmental complex, not as a separate “microthorax.”

A comparative hypothesis

Did the pleuron originate from the proximal limb base?

The subcoxal hypothesis interprets the thoracic side wall as proximal appendicular tissue incorporated around the coxa.

`Scx`
Subcoxa — a proposed proximal limb-base region incorporated into the body wall.
`Apl`
Anapleurite — the dorsal supracoxal arch.
`Cxpl`
Coxopleurite — the supracoxal arch adjacent to the coxa.
`Spl`
Sternopleurite — the infracoxal arc below the coxa.
`Cx`
Coxa — the basal leg segment articulated to the thoracic wall.
`Sp`
Spiracle — an opening of the tracheal system in the lateral body wall.
Function

Three arcs brace the coxal opening and provide dorsal and ventral pivot points for leg movement.

Evolution

This is a reconstructed model; modern developmental evidence supports proximal limb contributions but refines the pathway.

Comparative evidence

Separate elements persist above the coxa

Protura and Collembola show distinguishable supracoxal sclerites that help test the generalized subcoxal model.

`Apl`
Anapleurite — the more dorsal supracoxal sclerite.
`Cxpl`
Coxopleurite — the sclerite immediately adjacent to the coxal rim.
`Scx₁–₃`
Subcoxal regions — proposed proximal limb-base areas in the three thoracic segments.
`Tr–Ptar`
Distal leg series — trochanter, femur, tibia, tarsus, and pretarsus.
Function

The supracoxal plates support and constrain rotation of the coxa at its thoracic articulation.

Evolution

Living apterygote hexapods are comparative models, not unchanged ancestors; positional correspondence supplies the evidence.

A transitional comparison

Stonefly nymphs retain separable pleural elements

External and internal views reveal how separate supracoxal plates become structurally connected in pterygote insects.

`T₁`
Pronotum — the dorsal plate of the prothorax.
`Apl`
Anapleurite — the dorsal supracoxal element.
`Cxpl`
Coxopleurite — the element bordering the dorsal coxal articulation.
`Tn`
Trochantin — a precoxal sclerite derived from the anterior coxopleurite.
`PlR`
Pleural ridge — an internal support visible especially in the internal view.
Function

The trochantin and pleural ridge stabilize the anterior coxal articulation and receive leg muscles.

Evolution

This condition bridges the comparison between separate supracoxal arches and a consolidated adult pleuron.

From arches to a load-bearing wall

The pleural ridge connects wing and leg mechanics

A consolidated pleuron creates a structural pathway from the wing fulcrum above to the coxal articulation below.

`Eps`
Episternum — the pleural area anterior to the pleural suture.
`Epm`
Epimeron — the pleural area posterior to the pleural suture.
`PlS`
Pleural suture — the external groove over the internal pleural ridge.
`WP`
Pleural wing process — the lateral fulcrum of the wing hinge.
`Prcx/Pcx`
Pre-/postcoxal bridges — connections to the sternum before and behind the coxa.
`Ls`
Laterosternite — a lateral sternal component interpreted as incorporated sternopleurite.
Function

The pleural ridge braces the body wall against alternating loads from wing strokes and leg reactions.

Evolution

Fusion and expansion of supracoxal elements produce episternal, epimeral, and coxal-supporting regions.

Internal skeletal support

The furca braces the ventral thorax

The insect endoskeleton is formed largely by inward folds and projections of the exoskeleton.

`SA`
Sternal apophysis — one of a pair of internal arms arising from the eusternum.
`Fu`
Furca — a forked endoskeletal structure formed when the apophyses share a median base.
`PlA`
Pleural apophysis — an internal arm of the pleural ridge.
`CxP`
Pleural coxal process — the pleural projection articulating with the coxa.
Function

The furca anchors ventral and leg muscles; connections to pleural apophyses create a transverse brace.

Evolution

Median inflection and fusion transform separate sternal arms into a stronger forked support.

A composite ventral plate

The adult sternum contains several structural sources

The definitive sternum may combine the primitive sternum, infracoxal elements, and an intersegmental sclerite.

`Stn`
Primitive sternum — the original intrasegmental ventral sclerotization.
`Ls`
Laterosternite — a lateral component interpreted as incorporated sternopleurite.
`Ss`
Spinasternum — an intersegmental ventral sclerite bearing a spina.
`Bs`
Basisternum — the principal area anterior to the sternacostal suture.
`Sl`
Sternellum — the eusternal area posterior to that suture.
`Prs`
Presternum — a narrow anterior area when separated by a submarginal groove.
Function

A composite sternum distributes leg and ventral muscle forces across a broad, reinforced surface.

Evolution

Incorporation and fusion change apparent plate boundaries without eliminating their inferred homologies.

Why one plate can look like many

Membranization can subdivide an ancestral sclerite

Separate-looking adult plates may result from loss of hardening within an originally continuous region.

`Bs₁–₃`
Basisterna — anterior regions of the three thoracic eusterna.
`Sl₁–₃`
Sternella — posterior regions of the corresponding eusterna.
`SA₁–₃`
Sternal apophyses — internal paired arms marked externally by pits.
`ES`
Eusternum — the intrasegmental ventral plate excluding the spinasternum.
`spn`
Spina — the median apodeme of a spinasternum.
Function

Membranous zones increase local flexibility while retained sclerites continue to carry muscle forces.

Evolution

Desclerotization, not only fusion, can generate the diverse plate patterns seen among insects.

Different shapes, shared plan

Sterna respond to different flight architectures

Fusion, displacement, and elongation can strongly alter the ventral thorax while preserving recognizable components.

`Bs₂/₃`
Meso-/metabasisternum — anterior eusternal regions.
`Sl₂/₃`
Meso-/metasternellum — posterior eusternal regions.
`Fu₂/₃`
Thoracic furcae — internal forked supports.
`Fss`
Furcasternum — a distinct sternal region bearing a furca.
`Sp₂/₃`
Thoracic spiracles — respiratory openings, distinct from `Spn`.
Function

The enlarged fly mesothorax and extended furcal ridge increase rigidity and muscle attachment for flight.

Evolution

Dipteran specialization emphasizes T₂ while reducing and consolidating T₃; cicadas retain a different balance.

The integration slide

A wing-bearing segment is a three-dimensional frame

Notal, pleural, and sternal structures form one mechanical unit around the wing hinge and coxal cavity.

`AN`
Alinotum — the main wing-bearing dorsal plate.
`PN`
Postnotum — the posterior phragma-bearing plate associated with the wing unit.
`ANP/PNP`
Notal wing processes — dorsal supports for axillary sclerites.
`Ba/Sa`
Basalare/subalare — pleural sclerites receiving direct wing muscles.
`WP`
Pleural wing process — the lateral wing fulcrum.
`ES`
Eusternum — the principal intrasegmental ventral plate.
Function

Wing loads pass through notal processes and the pleural fulcrum into the reinforced thoracic box.

Evolution

Flight required coordinated specialization of the dorsum, pleuron, venter, and their muscle attachments.

Segmental origin vs. functional unit

Postnota reorganize the dorsal thorax for flight

The dominant wing pair determines where the largest dorsal plates, phragmata, and longitudinal muscles develop.

`AN₂/₃`
Meso-/meta-alinotum — the principal wing-bearing plates.
`PN₂/₃`
Meso-/metapostnotum — phragma-bearing plates paired functionally with the preceding alinota.
`1Ph–3Ph`
Phragmata — plate-like internal apodemes supporting longitudinal muscles.
`DMcl`
Dorsal longitudinal muscles — indirect flight muscles spanning phragmata.
`Mb′`
Secondary membrane — a new functional separation behind a postnotum.
Function

Muscle tension deforms a consolidated dorsal box instead of merely pulling adjacent segments together.

Evolution

Hindwing-dominant taxa emphasize T₃; Diptera strongly enlarge T₂ and reduce the metathorax.

A counterintuitive homology

The postnotum comes from the following tergum

Functional association does not always reveal developmental or segmental origin.

`AN₂`
Mesothoracic alinotum — the forewing-bearing plate.
`PN₂`
Mesopostnotum — functionally mesothoracic but derived from anterior T₃.
`AN₃`
Metathoracic alinotum — the hindwing-bearing plate.
`PN₃`
Metapostnotum — functionally metathoracic but derived from anterior abdominal segment I.
`atg/acs`
Acrotergite/antecostal suture — landmarks used to trace the primary boundary.
Function

Each postnotum carries a phragma and completes the rigid box surrounding a major longitudinal flight muscle.

Evolution

Secondary membranes reassociate anterior parts of following segments with the wing unit in front.

Apply the model

Abstract plates become recognizable anatomy

The scorpionfly shows the generalized notal, pleural, and postnotal regions in the context of a complete insect thorax.

`Cvx/cv`
Cervix/cervical sclerite — the neck and its lateral support plate.
`T₁`
Pronotum — the independent dorsal plate of the prothorax.
`AN₂/₃`
Alinota — the two wing-bearing dorsal plates.
`PN₂/₃`
Postnota — the corresponding phragma-bearing plates.
`Eps₂/₃`
Episterna — anterior pleural regions of T₂ and T₃.
`Epm₂/₃`
Epimera — posterior pleural regions of T₂ and T₃.
Function

T₁ remains mobile for head and foreleg action, while T₂ and T₃ form a more integrated flight unit.

Evolution

The enlarged PN₃ illustrates incorporation of a substantial anterior portion of abdominal tergum I.

Surface topology has mechanics

Sutures map the reinforcing ridges beneath them

The alinotum is divided into named regions by grooves that often correspond to load-bearing internal ridges.

`Prsc`
Prescutum — the anterior region behind the antecostal suture.
`Sct`
Scutum — the major anterior portion of the alinotum.
`Scl`
Scutellum — the posterior portion behind the V-shaped suture.
`ts/TR`
Prescutal suture/ridge — corresponding external and internal structures.
`vs/VR`
Scutoscutellar suture/V-ridge — the boundary and brace between scutum and scutellum.
`ANP/PNP`
Notal wing processes — supports for the wing-base axillary sclerites.
Function

Ridges stiffen the notum, localize deformation, and help transmit muscle force to the wing hinge.

Evolution

Changes in ridge position and strength accompany different flight mechanics and wing-base geometries.

Comparison, not memorization

Similar grooves are not always homologous

Taxonomically useful surface patterns must be interpreted using position, internal ridges, muscles, and the wing base.

`vs`
Scutoscutellar suture — the primary landmark separating scutum and scutellum.
`no`
Notaulix — one of paired grooves converging posteriorly in the anterior mesoscutum.
`pf`
Parapsidal furrow — a lateral groove in the posterior mesoscutum of some Hymenoptera.
`rvs`
Reversed V-suture — a secondary V-shaped line with a posteriorly directed apex.
`f/l`
Transscutellar/transscutal sutures — derived transverse subdivisions in particular orders.
Function

Some sutures mark internal braces; others are secondary subdivisions with weaker mechanical significance.

Evolution

Independent modifications can produce convergent patterns, so superficial resemblance alone cannot establish homology.

Specialization for flight

The pleuron can be radically reorganized

A common pleural framework remains detectable even after strong subdivision, fusion, and displacement.

`PlS₂/₃`
Pleural sutures — grooves dividing meso-/metaepisternum from epimeron.
`WP₂/₃`
Pleural wing processes — lateral wing fulcra.
`Ba/Sa`
Basalare/subalare — attachment sclerites for direct wing muscles.
`Prcx/Pcx`
Pre-/postcoxal bridges — pleural-sternal connections around the coxa.
`Mer`
Meron — a posterior coxal region that may become incorporated into the pleural wall.
Function

In higher Diptera, an enlarged mesopleuron supports forewing power while a reduced metapleuron reflects hindwing transformation.

Evolution

Sutural bending, regional subdivision, muscle migration, and meron incorporation create the derived fly condition.

The skeleton becomes a machine

Thoracic muscles integrate flight and walking

Muscle groups are best understood by their origins, insertions, and effects on the thoracic box, wing hinge, or coxa.

`A/C`
Dorsal and tergosternal muscles — major indirect muscles that deform the thoracic box.
`B`
Tergopleural muscles — muscles connecting the notum with the pleuron.
`D`
Tergal wing muscles — direct muscles inserted on axillary sclerites.
`E′/E″`
Basalar/subalar muscles — direct wing muscles attached to pleural sclerites.
`I–N`
Leg-base muscles — promotors, remotors, pleurocoxal muscles, and the coxal adductor.
`P`
Extracoxal trochanter depressor — a thoracic muscle passing through the coxa to the trochanter.
Function

Indirect muscles power the stroke by deforming the thorax; direct muscles tune wing rotation, angle, and folding.

Evolution

Flight evolved by reorganizing an existing segmental muscle plan and adding specialized wing-base insertions.

From diagram to three-dimensional anatomy

Muscles connect the exoskeleton into a functional system

The external plates define the animal's form, but their internal surfaces, ridges, and apodemes provide the attachment sites that generate movement.

External habitus
The complete external body form; panel (a) shows the sclerites, tagmata, and appendage bases.
Sagittal bisection
A longitudinal division along the body axis; panel (b) exposes the internal muscles and their skeletal connections.
Muscle origin
The usually broader or more stable attachment from which a muscle is described as acting.
Muscle insertion
The attachment on the structure that is typically displaced during contraction.
Apodeme
An inward cuticular ridge, plate, or process—such as a phragma or furca—that increases attachment area.
Indirect flight muscle
A large muscle that moves the wings by deforming the thoracic box rather than inserting directly on the wing.
Follow the connections

Large thoracic muscles attach to the notum and internal apodemes; leg muscles connect pleural and sternal supports to the coxa and trochanter; abdominal muscles link successive terga and sterna.

Functional evolution

The mesothorax is enlarged to house the flight motor, while the abdomen retains a serial muscle pattern for ventilation, telescoping, and visceral movement.

Functional synthesis

How does the thoracic skeleton transmit force?

MuscleDorsal longitudinal and dorsoventral muscles deform the thoracic box.
ApodemePhragmata, ridges, apophyses, and the furca distribute tension.
NotumControlled deformation raises or lowers the wing-base supports.
Wing hingeNotal processes, axillary sclerites, and the pleural fulcrum redirect motion.
Wing or legThe appendage moves while the pleuron and sternum resist reaction forces.

Indirect wing control

Large muscles change thoracic shape without inserting directly on the wing.

Direct wing control

Basalar, subalar, and axillary muscles adjust wing rotation, stroke angle, and folding.

Leg control

Muscles arising on the notum, pleuron, sternum, and apodemes move the coxa and trochanter.

Evolutionary synthesis

Transformation, not replacement

Thoracic diversity is produced mainly by modifying a shared set of structural elements.

F
FusionSeparate sclerites unite into larger load-bearing plates.
M
MembranizationLoss of hardening creates flexibility or apparently separate plates.
D
DisplacementSpiracles, muscles, and boundaries shift while retaining historical identity.
R
ReassignmentStructures derived from one segment join the functional unit of another.

A modern caution about wing origins

Snodgrass's diagrams are valuable comparative models, but they should not be presented as a fully established linear history.

Developmental evidence strongly associates the wing blade with lateral tergal tissue, while proximal appendicular or pleural tissues may have contributed to the hinge and its musculature.

The precise evolutionary sequence remains actively discussed; label reconstructions as hypotheses and separate evidence from inference.

Suggested reading: Mashimo et al. 2017; Clark-Hachtel & Tomoyasu 2020; Ohde, Mito & Niimi 2022.

Chapter summary

From a generalized segment to a flight machine

Notum

The alinotum, postnotum, notal wing processes, and phragmata support the wing hinge and indirect flight muscles.

Pleuron

The pleural ridge connects the wing fulcrum to the coxal articulation, forming the principal lateral load path.

Sternum

The eusternum, spinasternum, apophyses, and furca brace the ventral thorax and anchor leg and body muscles.

The key morphological skill is to combine external sutures with internal ridges, articulations, muscles, development, and comparison across taxa.
