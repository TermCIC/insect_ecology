🌱 Biocontrol Farm

You run an 8 × 8 vegetable farm for one 120-day season. Plant crops, let them grow, and harvest them for money. Winged aphids and egg-laying armyworm moths fly in from the landscape and build up stage-structured populations on your plants.

Wild predators (ladybirds) and parasitoids (Aphidius and Trichogramma wasps) arrive too, but usually late. You can buy and release them, and plant shelter plants (flower strips, beetle banks) that raise the number of natural enemies each tile can hold (their carrying capacity, K).

Everything is drag-and-drop. Drag an icon from the bottom dock onto a tile. Crops and the harvest basket can be swept across several tiles in one drag.
Drag on empty ground to rotate the view, right-drag to pan, scroll to zoom. Click a tile to scout it.

On a phone: tap a tool to pick it up, then tap tiles to use it (tap ✕ to put it down), or drag it; the drop point sits just above your finger. Pinch to zoom, drag with two fingers to pan. Reach the money goal at any time to jump to the next level.

📖 Field guide

How to play

  • 🥬 Cabbage ($10, 40 days, sells for up to $45) and 🍅 Tomato ($20, 70 days, up to $110). The sale price is multiplied by crop health. Mature crops start to rot 12 days after ripening.
  • 🧺 Harvest ripe crops (they show a gold ring). ⛏️ Dig clears a tile ($2, free if the plant is dead).
  • 🌼 Flower strip and 🌾 Beetle bank take up a tile (they can replace a crop). They raise natural-enemy carrying capacity K within 2 tiles and attract wild enemies earlier in the season.
  • 🐞 🐝 🥚 Buy and release natural enemies onto a tile. They spread out on their own.
  • 🪤 Pheromone trap ($10) records moth flights and halves egg laying within 1 tile (mating disruption). 🔍 Scout shows a tile's ET/EIL decision.
  • 🦠 Bt ($10) is a selective biopesticide that kills caterpillars only and spares natural enemies. 🧪 Broad-spectrum ($8) kills aphids and caterpillars but also about 95% of natural enemies, leaves a 6-day residue, and builds resistance every time you use it.
  • Keyboard: Space pause, 1–3 speed, G guide, Esc cancel a drag.

Functional response: how killing rate depends on prey density

A functional response (Holling 1959) is the number of prey one natural enemy attacks per day, as a function of prey density N. Each enemy in this game follows its own curve:

Ladybird (Type II) Aphidius (Type III) Trichogramma (Type II)
  • Type II: f(N) = aN / (1 + a·h·N). Search is efficient, but the time spent handling each prey caps intake. When prey are scarce it kills a large share of them. During an outbreak it saturates, so the share it kills falls (inverse density dependence).
  • Type III: f(N) = bN² / (1 + b·h·N²). The wasp hardly bothers with rare hosts (learning, switching, refuges). Its killing rate rises with density before saturating, which is density-dependent and stabilising.
  • Numerical response: a ladybird needs many prey to produce one offspring (about 1 juvenile per 30 prey, 18-day development), so its population grows slowly. A parasitoid turns each host into offspring (a mummy, or a black parasitised egg) and closely tracks the pest after a 9–10 day delay.
  • Carrying capacity (K): natural enemies only recruit up to K on each tile. Nectar and pollen from flower strips (for parasitoids and ladybirds) and refuge and alternative prey on beetle banks (for predators) raise K and survival. Use the Enemy K view to see it.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

IPM combines tactics in order of preference, and uses chemicals only when monitoring shows they pay for themselves.

🧪 Broad-spectrum chemicals: last resort
🦠 Selective control: Bt, pheromone mating disruption
🐞 Biological control: conservation, augmentation, wild enemies
📊 Decision rules: economic threshold (ET) and injury level (EIL)
🔍 Monitoring: scouting, 🪤 pheromone traps, degree-day forecasts
🌼 Prevention: habitat for enemies (flower strips, beetle banks), crop timing
  • Economic injury level (Pedigo): EIL = C / (V · D · K · T), the pest density at which the damage prevented equals the cost of control. C = $8 spray, V = crop value × health, D = 0.00006 health lost per aphid per day, K = spray efficacy, T = days until harvest (max 20). Close to harvest T is small, so the EIL rises and spraying no longer pays.
  • Economic threshold (action threshold): ET = EIL × e−0.15 × 3 ≈ 0.64 × EIL. You act at the ET, not the EIL, because a growing pest population would pass the EIL before control takes effect.
  • Aphid-equivalents: one feeding caterpillar injures the crop as much as 50 aphids, so both pests are measured on one scale.
  • Enemy-adjusted threshold: above the ET, first compare the daily kill by natural enemies (from their functional responses) with the pest's daily growth. If the enemies are winning, hold off, because a spray would remove free control.
  • Degree-days: insect development depends on temperature. Each day adds (T − 10 °C) degree-days, and a moth egg needs about 64 DD to hatch. The pheromone-trap biofix (first moth flight) plus the temperature forecast predicts when larvae will appear. Hot mid-summer days speed up every insect in the game.
  • Resistance: every broad-spectrum application raises resistance by about 10% of what remains (Bt by about 4%). It fades slowly as susceptible pests immigrate. Resistance lowers K, which raises the EIL. Calendar spraying builds it fastest.
  • Selectivity and resurgence: broad-spectrum sprays kill natural enemies more thoroughly than pests. Survivors rebound without their enemies (resurgence). Selective tools like Bt keep biological control intact.
